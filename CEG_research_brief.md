# Constellation Energy (CEG) — Research Brief
Prepared for The Signal · Data as of Friday, October 23, 2026 close · Prices intentionally relative, not absolute

---

## 1. What it does (beginner-proof)

Constellation is the largest owner of U.S. nuclear plants — about **22 GW across 14 stations and 25 units** — which it runs around the clock and sells the output from, either into wholesale markets or under long-term contracts, alongside a competitive retail electricity business serving ~2.5 million customer accounts; since closing the **Calpine acquisition in January 2026** it also owns a large gas/geothermal fleet, for **~55 GW of total capacity** and roughly 10% of U.S. clean generation.

The current investment story is the third leg: converting that existing nuclear output into 15–20 year contracts with hyperscalers. Deals signed to date:

| Customer | Size | Structure | Signed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Microsoft | 835 MW (Crane / Three Mile Island Unit 1 restart) | 20-year PPA | Sept 2024 |
| Meta | 1,121 MW (Clinton, IL) | 20-year PPA, starts June 2027, +30 MW uprate | June 3, 2025 |
| Unsigned/disclosed batch | ~920 MW (incl. 176 MW Walmart → 30 MW Dresden uprate) | 15–20 yr, begins 2029–2032 | Q2 2026 |
| Amazon | 690 MW, incl. 190 MW Calvert Cliffs uprate | 20-year PPA, >$3.0B MD investment, online 2030–32 | Sept 30, 2026 |
| Google | 890 MW of uprates across 11 units at 6 sites + separate 2,700 MW 15-yr supply | 20-year PPA, >$4.3B investment, first uprate 2028 | Oct 6, 2026 |

Contract book disclosure (company): **~30% of expected 2029 baseload clean-generation MWhs under long-term agreement by 2032**; average duration on the Q2 batch 18.5 years; **147 million MWh** of annual available nuclear generation in 2029. Constellation does *not* publish a dollar "backlog."

## 7. The strongest bear case

**The thesis: CEG's fleet is genuinely scarce, and the scarcity is real — but the price of that scarcity is set administratively, by politicians, three weeks before an election. You are paying a growth multiple for a return that is capped by regulation.**

Evidence, strongest first:

1. **The cap is binding and it is falling.** PJM cleared 6,831 MW *short* of reliability and still could not bid above **$325/MW-day**. The cap **declined 2.5% year-over-year** (from $333.44) because it indexes to a reference resource. Utility Dive reported that absent the collar the auction would have cleared near **$555/MW-day** and cost **$29.7B instead of $16.4B**. That gap — roughly 70% of capacity revenue — is value that exists in the market and is being legislated away from CEG and toward ratepayers. Every $1/MW-day on CEG's ~18-19 GW PJM position is only ~$6.9M of revenue (18,875 MW × 365 days × $1), so the collar is not a rounding error; it is the swing factor.
2. **The backstop procurement is a competitor, not a subsidy.** The RBP will contract up to **~6.8 GW of *new* supply** with terms of **up to 15 years** at a **$555/MW-day cap**, pay-as-bid. New entrants get 15-year above-cap contracts; CEG's existing fleet stays under the collar. If the RBP succeeds, it closes the shortage that gives CEG its pricing power. This is the mechanism by which the bear is *proven right by the company's own success story*.
3. **Cash conversion is thin at the peak of the capex cycle.** TTM free cash flow **≈$309M on $31.3B of revenue** (about 1%), against $4.3B of Google uprate capital, the Crane restart (backed by a $1.0B DOE loan guarantee) and $3.9B of committed growth capital, with net debt up from $5.4B to **$24.0B** in six months. Management's $8.4B FCFbG for 2026-27 excludes growth capex — so both the bulls' "$8B of free cash" and the bears' "$300M of free cash" are true statements about different things. The honest read: the dividend and buyback are being funded while the balance sheet absorbs the build.
4. **GAAP earnings are far below adjusted.** Q2 2026: **$1.42 GAAP vs $2.55 adjusted** — GAAP is 44% lower; FY2025 GAAP EPS $7.40 vs FY2024's $11.89. Hedge mark-to-market and trust gains/losses run through the P&L. A company that must explain a gap that wide between its two EPS numbers every quarter has an earnings-quality problem in a de-rating market.
5. **Execution risk on the very projects being capitalized.** PJM's Phase I study flagged Crane transmission upgrades running "as late as December 2030" until the FERC rights transfer fixed deliverability; the NRC operating license is still a 2027 event. **Citi's Ryan Levine** sized it: each month of delay ≈ **$0.09/share**, each $1M of recurring cost overrun ≈ **$0.04/share**, and he warns the permitting timeline "could slip to the right." Citi is Neutral at $297.
6. **Political risk is now the dominant variable.** Electricity bills are a midterm issue; the White House has a "Ratepayer Protection Pledge" that Constellation's own deal release cites as the framework it is aligning with; DOE is intervening at FERC for the first time in at least five years to push costs onto data centers; and PJM's own CEO frames the auction result as a consumer-cost problem. When the remedy for a reliability shortfall is *more administratively determined supply*, the merchant generator is the funding source, not the beneficiary.
7. **A structural floor that may not hold.** The nuclear PTC under Section 45U is the reason the downside case on the fleet works at all — the 10-Q describes it as providing "increasing levels of support as unit revenues decline." Any change to its level, duration or eligibility resets the floor under a large share of output.

**What would prove the bears right, specifically:**
- PJM's **Oct 29 refiling** preserves a cost-allocation framework that lets states or large loads procure around CEG, or FERC imposes its own design in Docket EL26-108;
- the **2029/30 BRA clearing at or below $325 again while the supply response finally shows up** (cleared new supply was only ~525 MW in 2028/29, so watch for a jump) — that would demonstrate the shortage is being solved with other people's megawatts;
- FY2026 adjusted EPS guidance being **cut below $11.00**, or the FY2029 "20%+ growth" framing being quietly re-based;
- Crane slipping past 2027, or an NRC/DOE order that strands restart capex.

**Fair counterweight (for balance in the piece):** FERC's Oct 9 rejection of PJM's large-load rules was read by Jefferies as *bullish* for existing IPPs, because it keeps data centers buying capacity rather than self-supplying; and ~30% of 2029 baseload MWh is already contracted at premiums Jefferies pegged at ~$20/MWh over market on the Meta deal. The bear case is a *price-cap* case, not a *demand* case.
