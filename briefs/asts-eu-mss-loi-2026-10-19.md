## 0. THREE CORRECTIONS TO THE WORKING PREMISE (read first)

1. **The LOI was announced Monday, Oct 19, 2026 — not Oct 18.** The BusinessWire release ID is date-coded `20261018409347`, but the dateline is "Oct. 19, 2026," StockTitan timestamps it 10/19/2026 07:25 EDT, and Deutsche Telekom's and Vodafone's own releases are dated 10-19-2026. Use Oct 19.
2. **AST SpaceMobile is not a signatory to the LOI and is not named in it as the satellite partner.** The LOI is among the four carriers only. Their joint statement says the constellation "will be provided by a satellite partner still to be selected," and that the LOI "does not replace existing satellite partnerships." ASTS's own release says it "welcomes" the LOI. Any sentence implying ASTS was named would be false.
3. **"More than 500 million mobile subscribers" is the size of the European market ASTS is addressing, not ASTS subscribers.** The PR wording: "coverage across Europe, a market with over 500 million mobile subscribers."

Two further traps: ASTS has **no paying service subscribers** yet (Q2 revenue is gateways + government milestones), and Deutsche Telekom is an **integration-test partner**, not a signed commercial D2D customer — DT plans to offer Starlink Mobile's D2D service in 2028.

## 1. ONE-PARAGRAPH COMPANY EXPLAINER (plain English)

AST SpaceMobile builds very large low-Earth-orbit satellites — the BlueBirds, with phased-array antennas up to about 2,400 sq ft — that act as cell towers in the sky, connecting directly to ordinary, unmodified 4G/5G smartphones with no app, chip or dish. It does not sell to consumers: it sells wholesale to mobile network operators, who keep the customer relationship and bill their own subscribers for coverage in dead zones, and it also sells gateway ground equipment and government/defense connectivity. Revenue today comes from gateway deliveries and U.S. government milestones, not subscriber service; the company is pre-commercial on its core service, with 60+ MNO partners covering roughly 3 billion subscribers and about $1.2 billion of remaining performance obligations. Its bet is that carriers would rather rent a neutral wholesale network than let SpaceX become a retail competitor — which is why four of Europe's largest carriers are now trying to buy the spectrum themselves and pick a satellite vendor afterwards.

## 5. MOAT EVIDENCE (sourced)

1. **U.S. regulatory rights are already granted, not aspirational.** FCC order DA 26-391 (Apr 21, 2026) authorizes 248 NGSO satellites and SCS operations on AT&T's 800 MHz A/B and lower-700 A/B/C blocks, Verizon's 800 MHz A/B, and FirstNet's 700 MHz — under spectrum-manager leases granted the same day. Term runs to Sept 19, 2039.
2. **It owns scarce MSS priority rights outside the U.S.** On Sep 25, 2025 ASTS acquired an entity holding ITU S-band priority rights for MSS at 1980–2010 / 2170–2200 MHz — the same band Europe is about to re-allocate — giving it "up to 60 MHz of mid-band satellite spectrum globally" (10-Q).
3. **It can tune across ~1,150 MHz of partners' spectrum**, which is the actual technical lock-in: the satellite meets carriers on their licensed bands rather than forcing a new band. Combined with S-band rights, that is the "combine operator spectrum with satellite spectrum" story in ASTS's own Oct 19 release.
4. **Carrier distribution with equity, not just contracts.** Nine strategic partners — AT&T, Verizon, Vodafone, Rakuten, Google, American Tower, Bell Canada, stc group, TELUS — several are shareholders; 60+ MNOs cover ~3B subscribers. AT&T and Verizon are investors; Bell describes "our strategic investment in AST SpaceMobile."
5. **Ground infrastructure is being built in Europe by a European entity** — Satellite Connect Europe (Vodafone/ASTS, Luxembourg) is investing in ground stations in Germany, UK, Spain and France, a control centre in Germany, a satellite production facility in Barcelona and a test lab in Málaga. That is the piece the four-carrier LOI explicitly wants to keep inside EU jurisdiction.
6. **Government optionality:** Rakuten/ASTS JV was preliminarily selected by Japan's MIC for the J-LEO initiative, "total expected value up to approximately $1 billion in non-dilutive, non-debt government capital" (Q2 PR).

## 6. BEAR CASE EVIDENCE (sourced, with numbers)

1. **The cash burn is enormous relative to revenue.** Q2 revenue $31.5M against $622M of quarterly capex and −$719M of free cash flow; H1 capex $859M. Pro forma liquidity of >$3.7B is roughly five quarters at Q2's FCF burn — before the $775–795M of purchase commitments and $80M/yr Ligado payment.
2. **The backlog is back-loaded.** Of ~$1.2B remaining performance obligations at Jun 30, only ~6.6% (~$79M) is expected to be recognized in the next 12 months — against FY26 guidance of $150–200M. Most of this year's revenue has to be newly won.
3. **Dilution is structural, and the converts are far out of the money.** Class A shares rose 5.0% in H1 alone; the $800M ATM was fully used and terminated in March; July added $1.15B of 1.625% notes with a $149.20 effective conversion price — about 3.6× today's $41.87 — plus $111.4M of capped calls. Noncontrolling interest is still ~23% of AST LLC.
4. **Launch dependency is also a competitor dependency.** SpaceX is ASTS's primary launch provider and now its most capitalised rival. Piper cut its 2026 satellite forecast from 45 to 18 and pushed U.S. commercial service from Q4 2026 to H2 2027, explicitly warning SpaceX "could restrict launch capacity" for ASTS. BB14–16 have sat at the Cape since Sep 30 with no date. Consultant Tim Farrar argues 60 satellites may slip to 2028.
5. **SpaceX's vertical integration is the structural threat.** It now has: FCC approval for 15,000 D2D satellites (Oct 6); ~650 D2D satellites operating today; V2 satellites claimed at 100× current bandwidth; a nationwide 800 MHz low-band layer (pending FCC approval); its own rockets; and an FCC waiver letting it be its own terrestrial licensee rather than needing a carrier partner. ASTS needs carriers to opt in; SpaceX needs them to opt out.
6. **Valuation leaves no room for the execution case.** $16.3B market cap and ~141× TTM sales on $115.3M of revenue, with zero service subscribers, negative gross-margin dynamics, and a live securities class action (lead-plaintiff deadline Nov 13, 2026; claims unproven) over disclosures about capital sufficiency and ~$3B of convertible offerings.

## 7. THE EUROPEAN SPECTRUM FIGHT — the concrete mechanism for the thesis

- The asset is the **2 GHz MSS band**: 1980–2010 MHz uplink / 2170–2200 MHz downlink, harmonised EU-wide, and explicitly described by the Commission as "the optimal spectrum for the deployment of innovative satellite D2D services" and "sufficiently large to support not only voice and SMS, but also broadband."
- **Current rights expire May 2027.** Two 2009 licensees hold 2×15 MHz each: Viasat (ex-Inmarsat) and EchoStar (ex-Solaris). EchoStar's European rights are the ones being transferred to SpaceX in the U.S. deal — which is why a European carrier consortium matters: it keeps that block out of Starlink's hands.
- **The Commission's proposed rule (COM(2026) 311, May 27, 2026)** creates an EU-level comparative selection for six paired 5 MHz blocks: two contiguous blocks for a secure government MSS/hybrid system that must integrate with **IRIS²** and be provided by an EU operator; one or more blocks reserved for a **"Union new entrant"**; and one or more open to Member State or third-country applicants. That is the legal basis for the carriers' "European new entrant" play — and the reason a U.S. satellite operator cannot simply bid alone for the best block.
- **The process is live, not hypothetical.** Parliament procedure 2026/0134(COD), ITRE committee, rapporteur Angelika Winzig; amendments tabled Sept 17, 2026; still awaiting committee decision. The EU is aiming to adopt the regulation by May 2027, with new licences from 2028.
- **It is now a transatlantic fight.** FCC Chairman Brendan Carr has said the U.S. would apply "reciprocity and nondiscrimination" limits to European satellite operators; U.S. Ambassador to the EU Andrew Puzder threatened retaliation "devastating" for European companies (FT). Commissioner Virkkunen has said she would oppose a consortium built around a non-European operator, and Macron has called for a fully European consortium. That is the central ambiguity in the Oct 19 LOI: the carriers want European governance, but the only near-term vendor with broadband-class D2D hardware at scale is American.
- **Why carriers are doing this at all:** in the words of Ookla's Luke Kehoe, "Europe's operators want to avoid becoming effectively MVNOs for satellite coverage, selling somebody else's service on somebody else's terms." That is the thesis in one quote: the moat is the spectrum licence and the subscriber relationship, not the satellite.
